## Summary

On January 27, 2010, Apple CEO Steve Jobs unveiled the first-generation iPad at a press event in San Francisco, describing it as a "magical and revolutionary device" that occupied a new "third category" of personal computing between smartphones and laptop computers. The Wi-Fi model went on sale in the United States on April 3, 2010, at a starting price of $499; a Wi-Fi + 3G variant followed on April 30.

The first-generation iPad featured a 9.7-inch multi-touch display, Apple's custom A4 system-on-chip processor, up to 64 GB of storage, and ran a modified version of iPhone OS (later renamed iOS). The device did not support multitasking, a physical keyboard, or a camera in its original form, but it could access Apple's App Store — already populated with hundreds of thousands of apps designed for the iPhone — from launch day.

Commercial uptake was immediate and unprecedented for a new product category. Apple sold more than 300,000 units on the first day, reached 1 million sales within 28 days (half the time the original iPhone had required), crossed 3 million by mid-June 2010, and sold approximately 15 million units before the iPad 2 replaced it in March 2011. The iPad commanded roughly 83 percent of all tablet computer sales in 2010. *Time* magazine named it one of the 50 Best Inventions of 2010, and *Popular Science* included it in their "Best of What's New 2010" list.

The iPad's success catalyzed a transformation in consumer electronics. Established manufacturers including Samsung, Motorola, and HP rapidly introduced competing tablets, making the touch-based tablet a mainstream product category. The device also significantly accelerated mobile application development, changed how consumers read news and books, and found widespread adoption in enterprise, education, and healthcare settings. As of 2022, Apple had sold more than 670 million iPads, cementing the company's position as the dominant tablet computer vendor.

## The January 27 Keynote: "A Third Category"

Steve Jobs unveiled the iPad at the Yerba Buena Center for the Arts in San Francisco on January 27, 2010, to an audience of press, developers, and invited guests. The keynote opened with Jobs challenging the audience to consider whether there was room for "a third category of device" between smartphones and laptops — noting that existing tablets and netbooks, which had attracted considerable investor and media attention in 2009, had failed to deliver a genuinely new usage paradigm. Jobs's specific criticism was that netbooks were "not better at anything" than existing devices.

Jobs demonstrated the iPad while seated in an armchair on stage — a deliberate staging choice intended to communicate relaxation and domestic comfort rather than the standing-at-a-podium formality of conventional product presentations. He showcased browsing the web, reading *The New York Times*, watching video, browsing photos, composing email, and running existing iPhone apps at their native size or doubled to fill the screen. He emphasized that the device "just works" as the first demonstration criterion, foregrounding the user experience rather than hardware specifications. The Wi-Fi version's $499 starting price — significantly lower than most analyst predictions of $700–$1,000 — was presented as the keynote's dramatic climax.

The presentation was widely regarded as one of Jobs's finest, and the clip of him collapsing the "best device" slide to reveal $499 in large text was replayed extensively across technology media and has become one of the defining moments in product announcement history.

## Developer Ecosystem and Content Partners

A critical factor in the iPad's commercial success — absent from earlier tablet efforts — was the existing iPhone app ecosystem. By January 2010, the App Store contained more than 140,000 applications, all of which ran on the iPad on launch day at native iPhone resolution. Many of the most popular categories — games, news readers, reference tools, productivity apps — translated immediately to the larger screen. Apple simultaneously announced that iPad-native apps would require the full screen and could not simply be stretched iPhone versions.

Apple negotiated content agreements with major publishers before the iPad's launch. HarperCollins, Penguin, Simon & Schuster, Macmillan, and Hachette signed distribution agreements for the new iBookstore, announced alongside the iPad in January. Apple's adoption of the "agency model" — in which publishers set retail prices — rather than Amazon's "wholesale model" disrupted the e-book pricing equilibrium Amazon had established with Kindle and later led to a Department of Justice antitrust lawsuit against Apple and five publishers (settled 2012–2014). The iPad's iBookstore arrival directly challenged the Kindle, whose Kindle for iPad application launched the same day as the device, demonstrating Amazon's recognition that the iPad was a serious threat to its e-reader business.

## Market Context and Competitor Response

At the time of the iPad's January 2010 announcement, the tablet computing market was dominated by two failed paradigms: Microsoft's Tablet PC line (introduced 2001, discontinued by most manufacturers by 2010), which ran full Windows on pen-input hardware and had failed to gain consumer traction, and the netbook category (small, cheap laptops popularized by Asus's Eee PC in 2007–2009), which had seen rapid growth but also rapid commoditization with thin margins and poor user experience.

Competitors reacted rapidly. Samsung introduced the Galaxy Tab (7-inch Android tablet) in September 2010, selling 600,000 units in the first few weeks of availability — significant by pre-iPad standards, but trivial by comparison with Apple's trajectory. Motorola's Xoom, HP's TouchPad, RIM's BlackBerry PlayBook, and a wave of Android tablets followed in 2011. None achieved meaningful market share against the iPad: as of Q4 2012, two and a half years after launch, Apple still held approximately 54 percent of the global tablet market.

## Significance

The iPad launch of 2010 established the modern tablet computer as a viable and mass-market product category, succeeding where earlier tablet attempts (Microsoft's Tablet PC initiative in the early 2000s) had failed. By combining an established app ecosystem with more than 140,000 applications at launch, intuitive multi-touch interaction, a competitive price point, and Apple's brand and retail network, the iPad created an entirely new market segment and triggered a restructuring of the personal computing industry. It accelerated the shift from desktop and laptop computing toward mobile, touch-based devices, influenced how media companies delivered digital content, and demonstrated that consumers would pay for curated, integrated hardware-software experiences.

The iPad's $499 entry price also influenced the netbook market directly: netbook sales, which had grown rapidly through 2009, began declining within months of the iPad's launch and had largely disappeared by 2013 as consumers chose tablets and smartphones over low-end laptops. The iPad also marked Steve Jobs's final major product category launch before his death in October 2011 — and arguably his most commercially durable: iPad shipments remained a significant Apple business unit more than a decade later.

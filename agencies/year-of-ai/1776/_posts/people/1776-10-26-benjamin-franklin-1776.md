## Summary

Benjamin Franklin, then 70 years old, served in 1776 as the American Revolution's most consequential diplomat. Over the course of the year he participated in four distinct critical roles: member of the Committee of Secret Correspondence (the proto-State Department), delegate to the failed Canada mission, member of the Committee of Five that drafted the Declaration of Independence alongside [Thomas Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}), and — as the year's most significant contribution — leader of the American diplomatic commission dispatched to France in October 1776. While [George Washington]({{ '/news/people/george-washington-1776/' | relative_url }}) fought the military campaigns that kept the Continental Army alive, Franklin's task was to secure the European alliance and materiel supply that made sustained resistance possible. His fame as a natural philosopher and his deep European contacts made him the only American whose personal reputation could command the attention of the French court.

### Committee of Secret Correspondence

The Second Continental Congress established the Committee of Secret Correspondence on November 29, 1775, to maintain clandestine communication with potential European allies. Its original members were Benjamin Franklin, John Dickinson, Benjamin Harrison, John Jay, and Thomas Johnson, with Robert Morris joining shortly after. Franklin was its most active member, leveraging contacts built during his two decades as colonial agent in London and his scientific correspondence with French philosophes. The committee's first European agent was Arthur Lee in London; in December 1775 it met clandestinely at Carpenters' Hall in Philadelphia with Julien Alexandre Achard de Bonvouloir, an unofficial French agent, to sound out France's willingness to provide covert assistance. Based on these overtures and Franklin's dispatches, French Foreign Minister Charles Gravier, Comte de Vergennes, authorized the creation of a fictitious commercial house — Roderigue Hortalez & Cie — under playwright and arms dealer Pierre-Augustin Caron de Beaumarchais. Funded with one million livres each from France and Spain in May 1776, the company channeled muskets, cannon, gunpowder, clothing for 30,000 men, and other military supplies to the Continental Army, shipments that proved critical to the Saratoga campaign of 1777.

In March 1776, the committee dispatched Silas Deane to Paris with instructions (drafted largely by Franklin) to approach Vergennes with two requests: a loan of arms and military stores, and assurance that France would recognize American independence if declared. Deane arrived in May 1776 and began secret negotiations that ran in parallel with the Hortalez & Cie arrangements.

### Canada Mission: February–May 1776

On February 15, 1776, Congress appointed a three-person commission — Franklin, Samuel Chase, and Charles Carroll of Carrollton — to travel to Montreal and persuade Canadian colonists to join the American cause or at minimum remain neutral. The commission also included Father John Carroll (later the first Catholic bishop in the United States), whose presence was intended to help reassure the predominantly Catholic Québécois population. The mission was a severe physical ordeal for the 70-year-old Franklin, requiring a winter journey of some 700 miles through wilderness conditions north of New York; Franklin was so debilitated by gout and boils that he had to be carried in a litter over portions of the route. The commission arrived in Montreal in late April but found that the Continental Army's chaotic occupation, its seizure of goods without payment, and its antagonism toward the Catholic Church had thoroughly alienated the local population. Franklin, weakened by the journey, returned to Philadelphia by late May, reporting to Congress that Canada could not be won by persuasion. The failure solidified his view that French military alliance, not Canadian accession, was the necessary strategic priority.

### Committee of Five and the Declaration of Independence

On June 11, 1776, Congress appointed Franklin to the Committee of Five alongside [Thomas Jefferson]({{ '/news/people/thomas-jefferson-1776/' | relative_url }}), John Adams, Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston to draft a declaration of independence. Franklin's advanced age and poor health led to Jefferson becoming the primary drafter; Jefferson later recalled that Franklin visited him twice in his lodgings on Market Street to review the evolving text. Franklin made several editorial changes to Jefferson's draft, the most celebrated of which may have been altering "sacred and undeniable" truths to "self-evident truths" — though historians debate the precise attribution of this change. The Declaration was adopted by Congress on July 4, 1776. Franklin was one of its 56 signatories when it was formally engrossed and signed on August 2, 1776.

### Peace Conference and Diplomatic Commission

Following the British victory at the [Battle of Long Island]({{ '/news/military/battle-of-long-island/' | relative_url }}) (August 27, 1776), British Admiral Lord Richard Howe paroled General John Sullivan and sent him to Congress with an offer of a peace conference. Franklin, John Adams, and Edward Rutledge met with Admiral Howe on Staten Island on September 11, 1776. Howe offered reconciliation under conditions requiring revocation of the Declaration of Independence. The American commissioners refused, and the conference ended without result — but it confirmed to London that the colonies were committed to full independence. On September 26, 1776, Congress appointed Franklin, Silas Deane, and Thomas Jefferson (Jefferson declined; Arthur Lee took the post) as Commissioners to negotiate a Treaty of Alliance and Treaty of Amity and Commerce with France.

### Public Diplomacy and the Paris Press

Franklin understood from his earliest weeks in France that his personal celebrity was a diplomatic instrument as potent as any formal credential. He cultivated French pamphleteers, philosophes, and journalists to sustain favorable coverage of the American cause in the Paris press. His letters and essays — circulated through Parisian salons and reproduced in French periodicals — framed the American Revolution as a vindication of Enlightenment principles: natural rights, commerce free from mercantilist restriction, and governance by consent. The contrast between his plain Quaker-style dress and the powdered-wig formality of Versailles was not accidental; Franklin carefully maintained his image as a republican sage, printing portraits and medallions of himself that sold briskly across Paris.

The Masonic lodges of Paris provided an additional channel of informal influence outside formal diplomatic protocol. Franklin was proposed for membership in the Lodge of the Nine Sisters (Loge des Neuf Sœurs) — whose members included the encyclopédistes Condorcet and Voltaire — and was formally admitted in April 1778. His Masonic networks in the months following his December 1776 arrival supplemented the official contacts managed through Vergennes. French public enthusiasm generated by this sustained press and social campaign created popular and parliamentary pressure that made indefinite denial of open alliance diplomatically untenable for the French crown, even before the Saratoga news arrived in December 1777.

The French financial commitment to the covert war — the million-livre contributions from France and Spain that funded [Roderigue Hortalez & Cie]({{ '/news/economics/roderigue-hortalez-company/' | relative_url }}) — was partly a product of this same diplomatic pressure. Franklin's cultivation of Vergennes and the wider French court was inseparable from the fiscal arrangements that kept the Continental Army in the field.

### Departure and Arrival in France

On October 26, 1776, Franklin sailed from Philadelphia aboard the sloop-of-war *Reprisal*, accompanied by his two grandsons — William Temple Franklin (who would serve as his secretary) and Benjamin Franklin Bache. During the Atlantic crossing, the *Reprisal* captured two British merchant vessels as prizes — an early indication of American naval aggression in European waters that signaled the war's global reach to the French court. After a stormy 30-day crossing, the party landed at Auray in Brittany on December 3, 1776 — the same week that [Washington's army]({{ '/news/people/george-washington-1776/' | relative_url }}) was completing its retreat across New Jersey — and traveled overland to Paris, arriving at the village of Passy (now part of Paris) where Franklin established his residence at the Hôtel de Valentinois, offered free of charge by a sympathetic French merchant. On December 28, 1776, Franklin had his first formal meeting with Vergennes, presenting the American commissioners' case for a formal French alliance. Vergennes, though sympathetic, demurred pending clearer evidence of American military viability — evidence that would arrive in December 1777 with news of the British defeat at Saratoga.

Despite the inconclusive diplomatic opening, Franklin's presence in Paris produced an immediate popular effect. His simple dress, fur cap, and bifocal spectacles contrasted theatrically with the powdered wigs and court finery of Versailles, and French society received him as a living embodiment of Enlightenment virtue — scientist, statesman, and republican philosopher in one figure. This celebrity status proved diplomatically useful, generating public pressure on the French government to support the American cause and frustrating British efforts to suppress French aid.

## Significance

Franklin's work in 1776 established the diplomatic and covert supply networks that sustained the Continental Army through its most difficult years. The Hortalez & Cie supply operation, developed in parallel with his committee correspondence, delivered materiel that historians credit as essential to the survival of the American cause in 1776–1777. His personal reputation in France created a political climate that made the Treaty of Alliance of February 6, 1778 — providing open French military, naval, and financial support — achievable. No other American figure possessed the combination of scientific fame, European social connections, and diplomatic dexterity that the mission required. His appointment as Minister to France in 1778 and his negotiation of the Treaty of Paris (1783) both flowed directly from the groundwork he laid in the closing months of 1776.

## Later Life & Legacy

Franklin's diplomatic mission, which began in October 1776 with his sail from Philadelphia and his arrival in Paris on December 3, 1776, extended far beyond the single year of his initial posting. His work in Paris over the next decade proved essential to American victory and to the founding of the new republic. In 1777, the news of the American victory at Saratoga, combined with Franklin's own cultivation of French sentiment, finally persuaded Versailles to move from covert support to open alliance. On February 6, 1778, Franklin negotiated the Treaty of Alliance with the French crown, committing France to military and naval support for the American cause — a diplomatic achievement that transformed the Revolutionary War from a colonial insurgency into a global conflict that overstretched British resources.

As the war continued, Franklin expanded his role as Minister to France, negotiating additional treaties of commerce and amity, and managing American diplomatic relations with other European powers. He remained personally popular in Paris, where his image as the rustic American sage — his famous fur cap and plain dress contrasting with Versailles finery — made him a symbol of republican virtue in the salons and pamphlets of the Enlightenment. The Treaty of Paris, signed on September 3, 1783, which formally ended the Revolutionary War and recognized American independence, bore Franklin's signature as chief American negotiator, alongside John Jay and John Adams. That treaty secured British recognition of American sovereignty and territorial claims to the Mississippi River — territorial gains that set the stage for westward expansion.

In 1785, at the age of 79 and with his health declining, Franklin returned to Philadelphia, where he was received as the senior statesman of the founding generation. The following year, in 1787, he was elected a delegate to the Constitutional Convention, where he served as Pennsylvania's representative. Though his physical infirmities confined him to a chair, his counsel was sought on matters of compromise and union. In debates over slavery, representation, and executive power, Franklin advocated for pragmatism and the subordination of particular interests to the common good — a voice of moderation that helped bridge the deep divisions threatening the Convention's success.

Franklin's final public service came in February 1790 when, as president of the Pennsylvania Abolition Society, he signed a formal petition to Congress calling for an end to slavery — one of the founding generation's most forward-looking statements on the institution, though it was rebuffed by slaveholding delegates just weeks before his death. He also remained scientifically productive to the end, conducting experiments on electricity and continuing his correspondence with European natural philosophers.

Franklin died on April 17, 1790, mourned across the new nation and in France as a founder whose long life had spanned from the colonial era to the establishment of the Constitution. His legacy encompassed diplomacy that won the Revolutionary War, commercial treaties that shaped early American trade policy, and a lifetime of scientific inquiry and civic virtue that made him, along with Jefferson and Washington, an exemplar of the founding ideal — the educated republican citizen-statesman. His participation in the Constitutional Convention provided crucial symbolic and practical weight to that document's legitimacy, a fitting capstone to the career he had launched in 1776 as the Revolution's elder statesman and diplomatic architect.
